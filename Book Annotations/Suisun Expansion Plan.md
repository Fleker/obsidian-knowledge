This is the first time in a century that anyone has tried to create a new walkable city of this scale.
To do so, the Plan takes inspiration from what works in other cities around the world — focusing on the pedestrian in all dimensions. The human experience is prioritized over the needs of vehicles — while still accommodating cars.

*****

People tend to walk when daily needs are nearby and reaching them is safe, comfortable, and enjoyable. Walkable cities offer access by proximity, not just mobility:
• Close + compact: Density puts more things within a 10-15 minute walk. If farther than that, people will typically find other ways.
• Amenity mix: A mix of uses enables easy access to daily needs like schools, shops, services, parks, cafes, restaurants, and bars.
• Interconnected: Small blocks and many intersections offer direct and convenient walking routes.
• Safe + navigable: Intuitive paths and a sense of safety, created by the presence of people and protection from traffic, invite everyone to walk and roll.
• Comfort + character: A pleasant walk feels shorter, so shade, greenery, and visual interest support the choice to go on foot.

By securing an approval for an expansion that will take 40 years to fully build out, the Suisun Expansion Specific Plan will provide a much-needed antidote to the lengthy approval times that California development is “famous” for, and a “pressure valve” to accommodate some of the desperate demand for both industrial space and homes in California.

Most of the Plan Area is dominated by flat, open grasslands, which have historically been used for grazing. The Plan Area does not have any forests, and has limited tree cover primarily from planted, non-native trees, such as eucalyptus. The existing topographical character, which currently consists of flat plains to the north, flood terraces along the length of the stream corridor, and gentle slopes to the south, shall be preserved with a balanced grading plan that will also enhance views.

In the 1960s and 1970s, Suisun City experienced rapid suburban growth as the San Francisco Bay Area’s suburban ring expanded to formerly rural Solano County. Most of that growth occurred east of the historic downtown in suburban-style single-family neighborhoods. By the mid-to-late 1980s, the city’s growth had begun to level off as the city ran out of land available for development, and a lack of significant economic investment led to the decline of the city’s historic waterfront and downtown area. 
Using redevelopment funding tools, the City invested $65 million in blight elimination and infrastructure improvements, including construction of the entire marina and promenade, new affordable housing, and several civic buildings and spaces. These revitalization efforts drove increased property values and a resurgence in economic activity in Downtown Suisun City.

*****

Suisun City was established in the 1850s during California’s Gold Rush era as a shipping hub for agricultural exports grown nearby. In 1869, the Transcontinental Railroad connected to Suisun City along a railway route linking the Bay Area, Sacramento, and the Sierra Nevada foothills. With the arrival of the railroad and stations at the nearby towns of Elmira and Vacaville, Suisun City’s agricultural export industry faced increased competition as farm exports shifted commerce from the Suisun Channel via the city’s wharf to transport via rail.

*****

In 2025, despite being one of Solano County’s oldest cities, Suisun City remains the smallest city in the county, covering a land area of just over 4 square miles and with a population under 30,000 residents.

A classic American grid provides the underlying street pattern for the Plan Area, with block sizes similar to cities small and large, from Rio Vista to Chicago. Gridded street patterns such as these have a strong history in urban design because of their flexibility, ease of navigation, and ability to accommodate change over time. Within the Plan Area, the grid is adapted and modernized by providing a network of finer-grained, slower-moving neighborhood streets in between the major Movement Streets that connect across the city.
The transit system is designed on an approximately one half-mile grid of transit streets. External transit to other parts of the region will run on rapid transit lines running north-south and east-west, crossing in Downtown.

Rowhomes are a traditional urban housing type that emerged and became popular in cities across the world. The rowhome strikes a unique balance, offering elements of privacy and individuality that attract many to a suburban home combined with the benefits of living in a denser community fabric. This is possible because rowhomes are spatially efficient — designed to share common walls with neighboring units, forming continuous blocks of compact residences. Rowhomes provide unique open space opportunities, including semi-private front yard or stoop space, a private or shared backyard, and even a roof deck. A beloved trait of rowhome neighborhoods is how each home activates the streetscape through separate entries — lining streets with semi-private stoops or front gardens that encourage residents to spend time in the public realm, supporting community interactions, and activating the street.

*****

While the Plan also accommodates larger footprint apartment buildings, it heavily features rowhomes as a time-tested building type.

*****

The Plan envisions neighborhood blocks subdivided into a variety of parcel sizes with a narrow frontage, lining streets with many entrances, and offering visual interest and frequent transitions.
Typical parcel widths will vary between 16 and 25 feet. Typical parcel depths will vary anywhere from roughly 40 to 120 ft to accommodate a variety in building type. The variety of small parcels allows for adaptation and change, making it possible to combine parcels to build larger homes, add units, or replace small buildings with minimal disruption. This will help the place evolve organically over time. The parcel pattern allows for rowhomes of single-family, duplex, triplex, or quadplex configurations, with the option to include an accessory dwelling unit (ADU) in deeper parcels, and multi-family buildings of apartments/condos from six-unit to large configurations

*****

Walkable cities have some commonalities when it comes to their block patterns:
• Smaller blocks and more intersections • Enough density to support local shops to be successful • Relatively narrow street widths and limited parking supply, which keep things close together and turn streets into “urban rooms,” which create the cozy feeling humans need to want to spend time there The “typical” neighborhood block in the Plan is designed to support the rowhome typology. On a typical block of roughly 4 acres, a rowhome approach can yield anywhere from approximately 50 single-family rowhomes to 130 units of an even mix of duplex and triplex rowhomes. This is compared to a typical density of approximately four to five detached single-family homes per acre in a suburban context, which is equivalent to 16-20 units of suburban fabric when using the same block size.

*****

The block size allows for deep parcels that have a backyard and room for a “carriage house” served by the rear alley – which can be an accessory dwelling unit, a parking garage, and/or a workshop.

A superblock is a group of approximately 16 “typical blocks”. Superblocks are bounded by “movement streets” that connect across the Plan Area. • Within each superblock, streets are called “Neighborhood Streets.” Neighborhood Streets are designed as slow-paced and serving only local traffic, where children can play, neighbors can gather and chat, and safety is prioritized. • The streets on the outside of superblocks are called “Movement Streets” – what other cities may call boulevards. 
These have been designed to be great, comfortable streets as well, while still facilitating traffic and transit movement. See Chapter 4 Transportation and streets for the full detail on how the streets work.

A neighborhood consists of two superblocks-an area of approximately one half-mile by one half-mile, with space for approximately 32 “typical blocks.” One of the central goals of the Plan is to provide a full set of amenities in every neighborhood. *****

A primary driver of the Plan is that all residents are within walking distance of a local shopping street. Each neighborhood that includes residential uses (i.e., not including Solano Foundry) incorporates a neighborhood retail street, adding variety and life to the city. Local shopping streets come in different shapes and sizes, and usually are classic, two-sided retail streets with multiple shops next to each other, built with taller ground-floor ceiling heights, and right up to the sidewalk. Shopping streets usually extend for at least a block on both sides of the street. Corner stores are also distributed throughout neighborhoods as additional retail. Coupling two neighborhoods together provides a retail street of three double sided blocks.
To support thriving retail across all neighborhoods, the densities in the Plan have been designed to ensure that a sufficient base of customers live within the catchment area of the shopping street

Transportation demand management (TDM) programs employ strategies to increase the comparable attractiveness of walking, biking, and transit, and to reduce unnecessary car uses. Successful TDM strategies deliver a range of benefits, such as:
• Reducing demand for parking thereby promoting more efficient land use 
• Reducing the amount of vehicle-miles traveled (VMT) by single-occupancy vehicles 
• Reducing peak period congestion 
• Reducing emissions per passenger-mile and thereby improving air quality 
• Promoting healthier lifestyles by increasing active transportation 
• Reducing the percent of household income spent on transportation

The Plan Area is envisioned to have an electric car-sharing program, providing residents and visitors access to a range of electric vehicle types on-demand, reducing unnecessary vehicle ownership. The Plan is based on a holistic “one water approach” that embraces the following planning principles:
• Maximize conservation with fixture efficiency and water-efficient landscaping, resulting in residential water uses that are about 40%1 less than Solano County’s current average.
• Use recycled water for certain non-potable demands.
• Embrace regenerative design and green infrastructure for passive design that provides beautification and ecological benefit while improving resiliency.
• Steward the natural environment by reducing impacts to sensitive ecosystems.

Recycled water is a critical part of the overall water supply, extending water resources by offsetting potable demand. The Plan will meet California’s stringent treatment standards for use in non-potable demands, providing beneficial use that, when combined with a significant amount of on-site storage, avoids the need for disposal at least through the 20 Year Plan.

The planned wastewater treatment system for WRRF-1 and WRRF-2 assumes that MBR technology (or equivalent) will be implemented to treat raw wastewater.
